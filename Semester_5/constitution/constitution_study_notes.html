Making of Constitution

Constituent Assembly, Drafting Committee, enactment, features, and sources

Constituent Assembly Formation

The Constituent Assembly was formed in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. It had 389 members (284 from British India, 93 from Princely States). Members were elected indirectly by the Provincial Legislative Assemblies using the single transferable vote system of proportional representation.

The first meeting was held on 9 December 1946, with Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha as the temporary Chairman. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was later elected as the permanent President of the Assembly.

Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)

The Cabinet Mission Plan of March 1946 proposed a three-tier federation consisting of Provinces, Provincial Unions, and the Union of India. It recommended a Constituent Assembly with 389 members to frame the Constitution. The Mission also proposed a federal structure with a weak Centre (Union) having only three subjects: Foreign Affairs, Defence, and Communications. The plan provided for a Constituent Assembly to draft the Constitution and also proposed an interim government (cabinet).

Objectives Resolution by Nehru

The Objectives Resolution was moved by Jawaharlal Nehru on 13 November 1946. It declared India to be a Sovereign Democratic Republic and laid down the objectives for the Constitution, including justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity. This resolution was adopted on 22 January 1947 and later became the Preamble of the Constitution.

Drafting Committee

The Drafting Committee was constituted on 29 August 1947 with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as the Chairman. Other members included N. Muthuranga Mudaliar, Alladi Krishnaswami Iyer, K.M. Munshi, Mohammad Saadullah, B.L. Mitter, and D.P. Khaitan. The Drafting Committee prepared the draft of the Constitution, incorporating inputs from various committees and debates.

Enactment and Adoption

The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949. It came into effect on 26 January 1950 — chosen to commemorate the declaration of Purna Swaraj (complete independence) by the Indian National Congress in 1930. The date 26 January 1950 is celebrated as Republic Day. India became a Sovereign Democratic Republic on this day, replacing the Government of India Act, 1935.

Features of Indian Constitution

  • Lengthiest written constitution: Originally had 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. Now has 470+ Articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules (as of 2024).
  • Federal with unitary bias: Division of powers between Centre and States (Union List, State List, Concurrent List), but Centre has overriding powers during emergencies. Strong Centre makes it quasi-federal.
  • Parliamentary form of government: Based on the British model. The executive is responsible to the legislature. Prime Minister is the real executive head.
  • Blend of rigidity and flexibility: Some amendments require simple majority (ordinary law), others require special majority (2/3 of present + 50% of total members). Some require ratification by states.
  • Secular state: No state religion; equal treatment of all religions.
  • Democratic republic: Government by elected representatives; head of state is elected (President).
  • Universal adult franchise: Every citizen above 18 years has the right to vote.
  • Independent judiciary: Separation of judiciary from executive; Supreme Court as the guardian of the Constitution.
  • Single citizenship: Indians are citizens of India only, not of individual states.

Sources of Indian Constitution

The Indian Constitution drew from various sources:

  • Government of India Act, 1935: Federal plan, emergency provisions, bicameral legislature, office of Governor — major structural source
  • British Constitution: Parliamentary government, rule of law, single citizenship, writs, Speaker's powers
  • US Constitution: Fundamental Rights, judicial review, impeachment of President, independence of judiciary
  • Irish Constitution: Directive Principles of State Policy, election of President, procedure for amendment
  • Canadian Constitution: Federal structure with a strong Centre, residual powers with Centre, appointment of Governors
  • Australian Constitution: Concurrent List, amendment procedure, joint sitting of Parliament
  • French Constitution: Republic, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity in the Preamble
  • Soviet/Russian Constitution: Fundamental Duties, social, economic, and political justice
  • South African Constitution: Procedure for amendment of the Constitution
  • Japanese Constitution: Procedure established by law (Article 21)
Key Fact: The Indian Constitution is the lengthiest written constitution in the world. It took 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days (total 165 days of debate) to complete. The Constituent Assembly held 11 sessions. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar is called the "Father of the Indian Constitution."

MAKING OF THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION

Background and Constituent Assembly: The demand for a Constituent Assembly was raised by Indian leaders since the 1930s. The Cabinet Mission of 1946 (comprising Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, and A.V. Alexander) proposed the formation of a Constituent Assembly of 389 members. Members were elected by Provincial Legislative Assemblies through single transferable vote system. The Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December 1946, with Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha as the temporary Chairman. After partition, the Assembly had 284 members. It held 11 sessions over 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days.

Drafting Committee: On 29 August 1947, a Drafting Committee was formed with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as Chairman and 7 other members: N. Muthuranga Mudaliar, Alladi Krishnaswami Iyer, K.M. Munshi, Mohammad Saadullah, B.L. Mitter, and D.P. Khaitan. The Committee prepared the draft of the Constitution incorporating debates from various other committees (Union Powers, Provincial Constitution, Fundamental Rights, etc.). Dr. Ambedkar's leadership was instrumental in resolving complex issues and debates.

Objectives Resolution: Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution on 13 November 1946. It declared India to be a Sovereign Democratic Republic. This resolution, adopted on 22 January 1947, later became the basis for the Preamble.

Sources of the Constitution: The framers borrowed from several sources: Government of India Act 1935 (federal plan, emergency), British Constitution (parliamentary government, rule of law), US Constitution (Fundamental Rights, judicial review), Irish Constitution (Directive Principles), Canadian Constitution (federal structure), Australian Constitution (Concurrent List), French Constitution (Liberty, Equality, Fraternity), Soviet Constitution (Fundamental Duties, social justice), and South African Constitution (amendment procedure).

Adoption: The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. India became a Republic on this day. The Government of India Act, 1935 was repealed.

SALIENT FEATURES

  1. Lengthiest written constitution: 470+ Articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules — the longest in the world.
  2. Federal with unitary bias: Federal structure (Union and State governments) but Centre has superior powers. During emergency, Centre can override states.
  3. Parliamentary government: Prime Minister-led executive responsible to Parliament. Based on British model.
  4. Blend of rigidity and flexibility: Can be amended by simple majority (ordinary law), special majority (most amendments), or special majority + state ratification.
  5. Secular state: No state religion; government treats all religions equally.
  6. Democratic republic: People elect representatives; President is elected head of state.
  7. Universal adult franchise: All citizens 18+ can vote.
  8. Independent judiciary: Supreme Court as guardian of the Constitution; separation of powers.
  9. Single citizenship: All are citizens of India, not of individual states.
  10. Fundamental Rights, DPSP, and Fundamental Duties: Comprehensive rights framework for citizens.

Fundamental Rights & Duties

Articles 12-35, six categories of rights, writs, Right to Education

What are Fundamental Rights? (Articles 12-35)

Fundamental Rights are the basic human rights guaranteed to all citizens by the Constitution. They are justiciable — a person can move the Supreme Court or High Court for their enforcement under Article 32 (Dr. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul of the Constitution"). Originally there were 7 Fundamental Rights. After the 44th Amendment (1978), the Right to Property was removed from the list of Fundamental Rights and made a legal right under Article 300-A.

Right to Equality (Articles 14-18)

  • Article 14: Equality before law — all persons are equal in the eyes of law. No arbitrary discrimination.
  • Article 15: Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Exceptions: women and children, Scheduled Castes/Tribes, socially and educationally backward classes.
  • Article 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment. No discrimination in public employment. Reservations for backward classes.
  • Article 17: Abolition of untouchability — untouchability is abolished and its practice is punishable by law.
  • Article 18: Abolition of titles — no titles (except military and academic) to be conferred by the State.

Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22)

  • Article 19: Six freedoms — (a) speech and expression, (b) assembly, (c) association, (d) movement, (e) residence, (f) profession.
  • Article 20: Protection in respect of conviction — no ex post facto law, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination.
  • Article 21: Protection of life and personal liberty — no person shall be deprived of life or liberty except according to procedure established by law. This is the most important and widest article. Through judicial interpretation, many implied rights have been read into Article 21 (right to privacy, right to health, right to education, etc.).
  • Article 21A: Right to Education — free and compulsory education for children aged 6-14 (added by 86th Amendment, 2002).
  • Article 22: Protection against arrest and detention — rights of arrested persons (right to be informed of grounds, right to consult a lawyer, right to be produced before magistrate within 24 hours).

Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24)

  • Article 23: Prohibition of traffic in human beings, forced labour, and child labour. Any contravention is punishable by law.
  • Article 24: Prohibition of employment of children below 14 years in factories, mines, and any hazardous employment.

Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28)

  • Article 25: Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion. Subject to public order, morality, and health.
  • Article 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs — manage its own affairs, own property, establish/maintain religious institutions.
  • Article 27: Freedom from payment of taxes for promotion of any particular religion.
  • Article 28: Freedom from attending religious instruction in educational institutions.

Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30)

  • Article 29: Protection of interests of minorities — any section of citizens residing in India having a distinct language, script, or culture has the right to conserve the same.
  • Article 30: Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions — minorities (religious or linguistic) can establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32 — Writs)

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights through writs. The Supreme Court can issue five types of writs:

  • Habeas Corpus: "To have the body" — produced before court. Protects against illegal detention.
  • Mandamus: "We command" — directs a public authority to perform a duty. Cannot be issued against private individuals or the President/Governor.
  • Prohibition: Issued by a higher court to a lower court to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction.
  • Certiorari: Issued by a higher court to quash the order of a lower court, tribunal, or quasi-judicial body acting in excess of jurisdiction.
  • Quo Warranto: "By what authority?" — enquires into the legality of a person holding a public office.

Right to Property

The Right to Property was originally a Fundamental Right under Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31. However, the 44th Amendment Act, 1978 removed it from the list of Fundamental Rights and made it a legal right under Article 300-A. Now it is not enforceable through the Supreme Court under Article 32, but the State cannot deprive a person of property except by authority of law.

Important: Only the Supreme Court and High Courts can issue writs under Article 32 and Article 226 respectively. High Courts have wider powers — they can issue writs not only for Fundamental Rights but also for any other purpose. Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right — it cannot be suspended even during an emergency (except during armed rebellion per Article 359).

Explain Right to Equality in detail

The Right to Equality is enshrined in Articles 14-18 of the Constitution:

  • Article 14: Equality before law — all persons are equal in the eyes of law. Prohibits arbitrary discrimination by the State. "Equality before law" means equal treatment in equal circumstances. "Equal protection of laws" means similar situations receive similar treatment.
  • Article 15: Prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Allows special provisions for women, children, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and socially/educationally backward classes (SEBC).
  • Article 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment. No discrimination in employment. Allows reservation for backward classes (up to 50% generally).
  • Article 17: Abolition of untouchability — practice of untouchability is an offence punishable by law.
  • Article 18: Abolition of titles — no titles (except military and academic) to be conferred. Title "Bharat Ratna" is not a title in the constitutional sense.

Exceptions: Reservation for SC/ST/OBC, special provisions for women and children, protection of backward tribes in Nagaland, etc.

5 Marks
What are writs? Explain different types

Writs are written orders issued by the Supreme Court (under Article 32) and High Courts (under Article 226) to enforce Fundamental Rights and provide legal remedies. The five types are:

  1. Habeas Corpus (You may have the body): The most powerful writ. It is issued to produce a detained person before the court and to free them if the detention is found illegal. It can be issued against both public and private authorities.
  2. Mandamus (We Command): A command to a public authority or lower court to perform a duty it is legally obligated to do. It cannot be issued against the President, Governor, or private individuals.
  3. Prohibition: Issued by a higher court to a lower court, tribunal, or quasi-judicial body to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or acting contrary to rules of natural justice.
  4. Certiorari (To be certified): Issued by a higher court to quash the order or decision of a lower court, tribunal, or quasi-judicial body which has acted without or in excess of its jurisdiction.
  5. Quo Warranto (By what authority): Issued to enquire into the legality of a person holding a public office. It prevents unauthorized persons from holding public offices.

Note: High Courts under Article 226 can issue writs not only for Fundamental Rights but for any other purpose as well. The Supreme Court under Article 32 can issue writs only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS (Articles 12-35)

Fundamental Rights are justiciable rights guaranteed by the Constitution to all citizens. They are enforceable by courts. Originally 7, now 6 (after removal of Right to Property).

1. Right to Equality (Articles 14-18):

  • Art. 14: Equality before law — all persons equal before law; no arbitrary discrimination.
  • Art. 15: No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Special provisions for women, SC/ST, and backward classes.
  • Art. 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment. Reservations up to 50% for backward classes.
  • Art. 17: Abolition of untouchability — punishable offence.
  • Art. 18: Abolition of titles (except military and academic).

2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22):

  • Art. 19: Six freedoms — speech & expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession.
  • Art. 20: Protection against conviction — no ex post facto law, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination.
  • Art. 21: Right to life and personal liberty — "procedure established by law." Broadly interpreted by courts (right to privacy, right to health, etc.).
  • Art. 21A: Right to Education for children aged 6-14 (86th Amendment).
  • Art. 22: Protection against arrest — right to know grounds of arrest, right to counsel, right to be produced before magistrate within 24 hours.

3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24):

  • Art. 23: Prohibits human trafficking, forced labour, bonded labour.
  • Art. 24: Prohibits child labour below 14 years in hazardous employment.

4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28):

  • Art. 25: Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion.
  • Art. 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs.
  • Art. 27: Freedom from taxes for promotion of any particular religion.
  • Art. 28: Freedom from attending religious instruction.

5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30):

  • Art. 29: Protection of language, script, and culture of minorities.
  • Art. 30: Minorities can establish and administer educational institutions.

6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32):

  • The "heart and soul" of the Constitution (Dr. Ambedkar).
  • Allows citizens to move the Supreme Court directly for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • Five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
  • High Courts can issue writs under Article 226 for FR and other purposes.

Directive Principles & Fundamental Duties

DPSP (Articles 36-51), categories, Fundamental Duties (51A), FR vs DPSP

Directive Principles of State Policy (Articles 36-51)

Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are guidelines for the State to govern the country. They are enshrined in Articles 36-51 of Part IV of the Constitution. Unlike Fundamental Rights, DPSPs are non-justiciable — they cannot be enforced by courts. However, they are fundamental in the governance of the country and it is the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.

DPSPs are borrowed from the Irish Constitution and are based on the concept of a "Welfare State."

Categories of Directive Principles

1. Socialist Principles: Aim at providing social and economic justice.

  • Article 38: State to secure a social order for the promotion of welfare of the people.
  • Article 39: Secure adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, protection of children.
  • Article 39A: Provide free legal aid to ensure equal justice.
  • Article 42: Just and humane conditions of work, maternity relief.
  • Article 43: Living wage, decent standard of life for workers.
  • Article 43A: Right to participate in management of industries.
  • Article 47: Raise the level of nutrition and public health.

2. Gandhian Principles: Reflect Gandhian ideology.

  • Article 40: Organisation of village panchayats.
  • Article 43B: Cooperative societies.
  • Article 44: Uniform Civil Code.
  • Article 46: Promotion of educational and economic interests of SC/ST and weaker sections.
  • Article 47: Prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health.
  • Article 48: Organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry; prohibition of cow slaughter.
  • Article 50: Separation of judiciary from executive.
  • Article 51: Promotion of international peace and security.

3. Liberal-Intellectual Principles:

  • Article 44: Uniform Civil Code for all citizens.
  • Article 45: Provision for early childhood care and education (0-6 years).
  • Article 48A: Protection and improvement of environment, forests, wildlife.

Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

Fundamental Duties were added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 (Swaran Singh Committee) under Article 51A. Originally 10 duties, the 11th duty was added by the 86th Amendment Act, 2002 (Right to Education). Total: 11 Fundamental Duties:

  1. To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions.
  2. To cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired the national struggle for freedom.
  3. To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India.
  4. To defend the country and render national service when called upon.
  5. To promote harmony and spirit of common brotherhood transcending religious, linguistic, regional, or sectional diversities.
  6. To value and preserve the rich heritage of India's composite culture.
  7. To protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife.
  8. To develop scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
  9. To safeguard public property and to abjure violence.
  10. To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity.
  11. To provide opportunities for education to one's child or ward between the age of 6 and 14 years (added by 86th Amendment).

Fundamental Duties are non-justiciable — they cannot be enforced by courts. However, Parliament can make laws to give them effect.

Difference between Fundamental Rights and DPSP

Fundamental Rights Directive Principles
Justiciable — enforceable by courts Non-justiciable — not enforceable by courts
Negative in nature — prohibit State from doing certain acts Positive in nature — direct State to do certain acts
Individualistic — protect individual rights Socialistic — promote welfare of the community
Based on liberal philosophy Based on socialist and Gandhian philosophy
Cannot be suspended during emergency (except Art. 20, 21) Not suspended during emergency (Article 31C applies)
Costly — require funds for enforcement Not costly — just policy guidelines

Conflict between FR and DPSP: Minerva Mills Case

The relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles has been a subject of debate. The Supreme Court in the Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) case held that there is a harmony between FR and DPSP. Both are complementary — Fundamental Rights ensure individual liberty, while DPSPs ensure social and economic justice. The Court struck down the 42nd Amendment which had given DPSPs primacy over Fundamental Rights, stating that the Constitution balances both — one cannot be given absolute primacy over the other. The basic structure doctrine was reinforced.

Key Case: In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Supreme Court held that the Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. Giving absolute primacy to one over the other would disturb the harmony of the Constitution. This case is a landmark in understanding the FR-DPSP relationship.

DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES OF STATE POLICY (DPSP)

DPSPs (Articles 36-51) are non-justiciable guidelines for the State. Borrowed from the Irish Constitution, they aim to establish a welfare state.

Categories:

  • Socialist: Article 38 (social order), 39 (adequate livelihood, equal pay, child protection), 39A (free legal aid), 42 (maternity relief), 43 (living wage).
  • Gandhian: Article 40 (village panchayats), 44 (Uniform Civil Code), 46 (SC/ST welfare), 47 (prohibition of intoxicants), 48 (cow protection, agriculture), 50 (separation of judiciary).
  • Liberal: Article 44 (UCC), 45 (early childhood education), 48A (environment protection).

FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES (Article 51A)

Added by 42nd Amendment (1976), extended by 86th Amendment (2002). 11 duties including abiding by the Constitution, cherishing freedom struggle ideals, upholding sovereignty, defending the country, promoting harmony, preserving culture, protecting the environment, developing scientific temper, safeguarding public property, striving for excellence, and providing education to children 6-14.

RELATIONSHIP: FR vs DPSP

Fundamental Rights are justiciable (enforceable), negative in nature, and protect individuals. DPSPs are non-justiciable, positive in nature, and promote social welfare. The Constitution makers intended both to be complementary.

Key Cases:

  • Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Held that FR and DPSP are not complementary — if they conflict, FR would prevail. Led to First Amendment (1951).
  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973): FR and DPSP are complementary; neither is subordinate to the other.
  • Minerva Mills (1980): Landmark case. Struck down 42nd Amendment which gave DPSP primacy over FR. Held that harmony between FR and DPSP is a basic feature of the Constitution. Absolute primacy of one over the other would destroy the Constitution's balance.

Union & State Government

President, PM, Parliament, Supreme Court, Governor, High Court, Judiciary

President of India

The President is the head of state of India, elected indirectly by an electoral college consisting of elected members of both Houses of Parliament and elected members of Legislative Assemblies of States (and UTs).

  • Election: By proportional representation through single transferable vote.
  • Tenure: 5 years. Eligible for re-election.
  • Powers: Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial, Emergency (Article 352, 356, 360), Diplomatic (treaties, international agreements), Military (Supreme Commander of Armed Forces).
  • Veto Powers: (1) Absolute veto (withhold assent), (2) Suspensive veto (return bill for reconsideration — except Money Bills), (3) Pocket veto (no action).
  • Impeachment: Can be removed for "violation of Constitution" by Parliament (special majority in both Houses). Initiated in either House.

Vice-President and Prime Minister

Vice-President is elected by an electoral college of members of both Houses of Parliament. He is the ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha. He acts as President during vacancies in the office of President.

Prime Minister is the real executive head of the government. Leader of the majority party/coalition in Lok Sabha. Advises the President on appointments (Governors, CJI, CAG, etc.). Heads the Council of Ministers. Coordinates between various ministries. Represents India in international forums.

Parliament — Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha

Parliament is the supreme legislative body of India, consisting of the President, Lok Sabha (House of the People), and Rajya Sabha (Council of States).

  • Lok Sabha: 543 elected members (max 552). Term: 5 years (can be dissolved). Speaker presides. Money Bills can only be introduced here. More powerful than Rajya Sabha.
  • Rajya Sabha: 238 elected + 12 nominated members. Term: 6 years (1/3 retiring every 2 years). Vice-President is ex-officio Chairman. Cannot be dissolved. Deputy Chairman elected from members.
  • Speaker: Elected by Lok Sabha members. Maintains discipline, decides on points of order, presides over debates. Deputy Speaker assists.

Supreme Court and Judiciary

Supreme Court is the highest court of India. Established on 28 January 1950. Consists of the Chief Justice of India and up to 33 other judges (currently).

  • Appointment: CJI appointed by President after consultation. Other judges appointed by President after consultation with CJI and senior judges (Collegium system).
  • Tenure: Up to 65 years. Removal by impeachment (special majority in both Houses).
  • Jurisdiction: Original (disputes between Centre and States), Appellate (from High Courts), Advisory (President can seek opinion), Writ jurisdiction (Article 32).
  • Judicial Review: Power to declare laws unconstitutional if they violate the Constitution.
  • Judicial Activism: Active role of judiciary in protecting rights — Public Interest Litigation (PIL), environmental activism, etc.
  • PIL: Any person can file a petition on behalf of those who cannot, for protection of public rights. Introduced in the 1980s by Justice P.N. Bhagwati.

Governor, Chief Minister, State Legislature, and High Court

Governor is the head of state at the state level (Article 153). Appointed by the President for 5 years. Holds executive power of the State. Summons prorogues and dissolves State Legislature. Can reserve bills for President's assent. Can promulgate Ordinances.

Chief Minister is the real executive head at the state level. Leader of the majority party/coalition in the Legislative Assembly. Heads the Council of Ministers. Advises the Governor.

State Legislature: Most states have a unicameral legislature (Legislative Assembly). Some have bicameral (Legislative Assembly + Legislative Council). Members of Legislative Assembly (MLA) are directly elected.

High Court: Highest judicial authority in a state. Consists of a Chief Justice and other judges. Judges are appointed by the President. High Courts have writ jurisdiction under Article 226. They can issue writs for Fundamental Rights AND for other purposes (wider than Supreme Court).

Comparison: President is the head of state (ceremonial); Prime Minister is the head of government (real executive). Governor is the state-level head of state; Chief Minister is the state-level head of government. The real power lies with the PM at Centre and CM at State level.

Who appoints the Governor?
The Governor of a State is appointed by the President of India under Article 153. The Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President (normally for 5 years). The same person can be appointed as Governor of two or more states.

What is the tenure of President?
The President of India holds office for a term of 5 years from the date of entering office. The President is eligible for re-election. The President can resign by writing to the Vice-President, or can be removed by impeachment for "violation of the Constitution."

Explain powers of President of India

Powers of the President of India:

  • Executive Powers: Supreme Commander of Armed Forces; appoints Prime Minister, Governors, CJI, judges, CAG, CEC, ambassadors. All executive actions are taken in the President's name.
  • Legislative Powers: Summons, prorogues, and dissolves Lok Sabha. Addresses the first session after general elections and the first session of each year. Can promulgate Ordinances when Parliament is not in session. Assent to bills passed by Parliament (can withhold, return for reconsideration, or withhold finally for Money Bills).
  • Financial Powers: Union Budget is presented in Parliament in President's name. Money Bills can only be introduced with President's recommendation. No demand for grant can be made except on President's recommendation.
  • Judicial Powers: Appoints CJI and other judges. Can grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment. Can consult the Supreme Court on questions of law or fact.
  • Emergency Powers (Article 352, 356, 360): National Emergency (Article 352 — war, external aggression, armed rebellion), State Emergency/President's Rule (Article 356 — failure of constitutional machinery in State), Financial Emergency (Article 360 — financial stability threatened).
  • Veto Powers: Absolute veto (withhold assent permanently), Suspensive veto (return bill for reconsideration — not for Money Bills), Pocket veto (no action, bill lapses).
5 Marks
Discuss the structure of Indian Parliament

Indian Parliament consists of three components: the President, Lok Sabha (House of the People), and Rajya Sabha (Council of States).

Lok Sabha (Lower House): Maximum 552 members (530 from States, 20 from UTs, 2 nominated by President if Anglo-Indian community not adequately represented). Currently 543 elected members. Term: 5 years (can be dissolved earlier by President on advice of PM). Presided over by the Speaker (elected from among members). Deputy Speaker assists. Speaker's decisions are final and binding. More powerful than Rajya Sabha — Money Bills can only originate here.

Rajya Sabha (Upper House): Maximum 250 members — 238 elected by State Legislative Assemblies (proportional representation), 12 nominated by President from fields of literature, science, art, and social service. Term: 6 years, 1/3 members retire every 2 years. Cannot be dissolved. Vice-President is ex-officio Chairman. Deputy Chairman elected from among members.

Joint Sessions: If a bill is rejected by one House, or there is disagreement on amendments, a joint session can be called by the President. Since Lok Sabha has more members (543 vs 245), it usually prevails in joint sessions.

Explain the structure of Indian Parliamentary System. Discuss the powers and position of President, Prime Minister, Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha), and the role of Supreme Court.

INDIAN PARLIAMENTARY SYSTEM

India follows the Westminster model of parliamentary government, where the executive is responsible to the legislature.

President: Head of State (nominal/ceremonial). Elected indirectly by an electoral college. Term: 5 years. Powers include: Executive (appoints PM, judges, governors), Legislative (summons/dissolves Parliament, assents to bills), Financial (budget in President's name), Emergency (National, State, Financial emergencies), and Veto powers. Acts on advice of Council of Ministers (except in certain situations).

Prime Minister: Real executive head. Leader of majority party/coalition in Lok Sabha. Advises President on appointments. Heads Council of Ministers. Coordinates between ministries. Key decision-maker. The PM's position is secure as long as he enjoys the confidence of Lok Sabha.

Council of Ministers: Cabinet Ministers (senior, head key ministries), Ministers of State (junior, may hold independent charge or assist cabinet ministers), Deputy Ministers. Collectively responsible to Lok Sabha.

Lok Sabha: 543 elected members, 5-year term, Speaker presides. More powerful than Rajya Sabha. Controls the executive through question hour, debates, motions of no-confidence, and budgetary control.

Rajya Sabha: 238 elected + 12 nominated, permanent body (not subject to dissolution). Represents states in the federal structure. Equal powers with Lok Sabha in most matters except Money Bills and no-confidence motion (Lok Sabha only).

Supreme Court: Highest court, guardian of the Constitution. Original, appellate, and advisory jurisdiction. Judicial review power. PIL mechanism for public welfare. Independence ensured through security of tenure, fixed salary, and removal only by impeachment.

Constitutional Bodies & Amendments

Election Commission, CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission, Article 368, amendments, basic structure

Constitutional Bodies

These bodies are created by the Constitution and their composition, powers, and functions are defined by the Constitution:

  • Election Commission (Article 324): Conducts free and fair elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President. Consists of Chief Election Commissioner and other ECs. Appointed by the President. Independent authority — cannot be removed except by impeachment like a Supreme Court judge.
  • Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) (Article 148): Supreme audit institution of India. Audits the accounts of the Union and State governments and public sector undertakings. Appointed by the President. Can only be removed by impeachment. Responsible to the President/Parliament, not to the executive.
  • Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) (Article 315): Conducts examinations for recruitment to All-India Services and Central Services. Chairman and members appointed by President. Independent body — removal only by impeachment.
  • State Public Service Commission (SPSC) (Article 315): Similar to UPSC but at the state level. Conducts exams for State Services.
  • Finance Commission (Article 280): constituted every 5 years to recommend the distribution of tax revenues between Centre and States. Chairman appointed by President.
  • Attorney General of India (Article 76): Highest law officer of the country. Right of audience in all courts. Advises the Government of India on legal matters.
  • Advocate General (Article 165): Highest law officer of a State. Advises the State Government on legal matters. Right of audience in State High Courts.

Constitutional Amendment Procedure (Article 368)

Article 368 provides the procedure for amending the Constitution. Different types of amendments require different procedures:

  • Simple Majority: Certain provisions can be amended by a simple majority (like ordinary laws) — e.g., creation of new states, changes in representation, changes in salaries of judges.
  • Special Majority: Most amendments require a special majority: (a) majority of total membership of each House, AND (b) 2/3 majority of members present and voting. This is the most common method.
  • Special Majority + State Ratification: Amendments affecting federal provisions (election of President, distribution of powers, Supreme Court/High Courts, amendment of Constitution itself) require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures in addition to the special majority in Parliament.

The Constitution can be amended by Parliament alone (no role for states in most cases), which makes it relatively flexible compared to the US Constitution.

Important Constitutional Amendments

  • 1st Amendment (1951): Added Ninth Schedule to protect land reform laws from judicial review. Restricted freedom of speech (added restrictions on free speech).
  • 42nd Amendment (1976): Called "Mini Constitution." Added words "Socialist," "Secular," "Integrity" to Preamble. Added Part IV-A (Fundamental Duties — Article 51A). Added Article 31C to give DPSP primacy over FR. Made President bound by Cabinet's advice. Extended term of Lok Sabha from 5 to 6 years during emergency. Many provisions were later reversed by 44th Amendment.
  • 44th Amendment (1978): Reversed most 42nd Amendment provisions. Removed Right to Property from Fundamental Rights (made it a legal right under Article 300A). Restored 5-year term for Lok Sabha. Limited the scope of emergency provisions.
  • 73rd Amendment (1992): Granted constitutional status to Panchayati Raj institutions. Added Part IX (Articles 243-243O). Three-tier system of Panchayats (village, intermediate, district). 1/3rd reservation for women.
  • 74th Amendment (1992): Granted constitutional status to Municipalities. Added Part IX-A (Articles 243P-243ZG). Urban local self-government.
  • 86th Amendment (2002): Added Article 21A (Right to Education for children 6-14). Added Fundamental Duty (11th) about providing education to children.
  • 101st Amendment (2016): Introduced Goods and Services Tax (GST). Added Articles 246A, 269A, 279A.
  • 103rd Amendment (2019): Provided 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) in educational institutions and public employment.
  • 104th Amendment (2020): Extended reservation for SC/ST in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for another 10 years (till 2030).

Basic Structure Doctrine — Kesavananda Bharati Case

The Basic Structure Doctrine was established by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). The Court held that Parliament cannot amend the "basic structure" or "essential features" of the Constitution. While Article 368 gives Parliament amending power, it cannot destroy the basic structure.

Basic features identified by the Court include: Supremacy of the Constitution, Republican and Democratic form of Government, Secularism, Separation of powers between legislature, executive, and judiciary, Federal character of the Constitution, Unity and integrity of the Nation, Welfare state, Judicial review, Harmony between FR and DPSP, Parliamentary system, Free and fair elections, Limited power of Parliament to amend, Rule of law, and Principle of equality.

The 13-judge bench (largest ever) ruled 7-6. Parliament later attempted to override this decision through the 42nd Amendment (1976), but the Minerva Mills case (1980) reaffirmed the basic structure doctrine, striking down the clauses that attempted to negate it.

Landmark Case: Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — The Supreme Court held by a 7-6 majority that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 does not enable it to alter the "basic structure" of the Constitution. This doctrine has since been the cornerstone of constitutional law in India, protecting the Constitution from totalitarian amendments.

Explain Constitutional amendment procedure

Article 368 provides for three methods of amendment:

  1. Simple Majority: Some provisions can be amended by a simple majority of Parliament (like ordinary legislation). Examples: creation of new states, changes in Lok Sabha seats, changes in High Court boundaries, changes in salaries of judges.
  2. Special Majority: Most amendments require (a) majority of the total membership of each House AND (b) 2/3 majority of members present and voting in each House. Examples: Fundamental Rights, DPSP, amendment of Article 368 itself.
  3. Special Majority + State Ratification: Amendments affecting the federal structure require: (a) special majority in Parliament AND (b) ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures. Examples: election of President, extent of executive power of Union and States, Supreme Court/High Courts, amendment procedure itself, representation of States in Parliament.

Notably, the Parliament alone can amend most provisions — states have a limited role. This makes the Indian Constitution more flexible than the US Constitution.

Explain Constitutional amendment procedure under Article 368. Discuss important Constitutional amendments (1st, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th) and the basic structure doctrine established in Kesavananda Bharati case.

ARTICLE 368 — AMENDMENT PROCEDURE

The Constitution can be amended through three methods:

  1. Simple majority of Parliament: For ordinary provisions (new states, parliamentary representation, etc.).
  2. Special majority of Parliament: Majority of total membership + 2/3 of present and voting. For most amendments including FR, DPSP, and Article 368.
  3. Special majority + State ratification: For federal provisions — requires ratification by at least half of State Legislatures in addition to special majority in Parliament.

IMPORTANT AMENDMENTS

  • 1st Amendment (1951): Added Ninth Schedule (laws placed beyond judicial review). Added restrictions on freedom of speech (Article 19(2)). Added Article 31A and 31B to protect agrarian reform laws.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976): "Mini Constitution" — added "Socialist," "Secular," "Integrity" to Preamble. Added Part IV-A (Fundamental Duties, Art. 51A). Made DPSP superior to FR (Article 31C). Extended Lok Sabha term from 5 to 6 years. Curtailed judicial review powers. Made President bound by Cabinet advice. Later reversed by 44th Amendment.
  • 44th Amendment (1978): Reversed 42nd Amendment provisions. Removed Right to Property from Fundamental Rights (became legal right under Art. 300A). Restored 5-year Lok Sabha term. Limited scope of National Emergency to "armed rebellion." Made elections a mandatory precondition for President's assumption of office after emergency.
  • 73rd Amendment (1992): Constitutional status to Panchayati Raj. Added Part IX (Articles 243-243O). Three-tier system, 1/3rd reservation for women, State Finance Commission.
  • 74th Amendment (1992): Constitutional status to Municipalities. Added Part IX-A. Urban local self-government. Similar provisions for municipalities as for panchayats.

KESAVANANDA BHARATI CASE (1973) — BASIC STRUCTURE DOCTRINE

In this landmark 13-judge bench case (7-6 majority), the Supreme Court held that:

  • Parliament's amending power under Article 368 does not include the power to destroy or alter the "basic structure" or "essential features" of the Constitution.
  • The Constitution has a "basic structure" which cannot be amended.
  • Judicial review is a basic feature — Parliament cannot take it away.

Basic features include: Supremacy of the Constitution, Republican and Democratic form, Secularism, Separation of powers, Federalism, Rule of law, Judicial review, Harmony between FR and DPSP. This doctrine was later reaffirmed in Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao (1981), and other cases.
