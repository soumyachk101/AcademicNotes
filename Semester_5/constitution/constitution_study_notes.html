Constitution Overview

What is a Constitution?

A Constitution is the supreme law of a country that defines the framework of governance, the fundamental political principles, and establishes the structure, procedures, powers, and duties of government institutions. It is the source of all other laws.

Constituent Power: The power to frame and adopt a constitution rests with the people (popular sovereignty).
Constitutionalism: Government is limited by law and must operate within constitutional bounds.

Historical Background — Government of India Acts

ActYearKey Features
Govt. of India Act1858Abolished East India Company's rule. Crown took over. Post of Viceroy created.
Indian Councils Act1909 (Morley-Minto)Separate electorates for Muslims. Beginning of communal politics.
Govt. of India Act1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford)Dyarchy at provinces. Bicameral legislature at centre. Separate provincial budgets.

The 1935 Act is the bedrock of the Indian Constitution — about 75% of it is borrowed from this Act.

The Constituent Assembly

  • Formed: Based on Cabinet Mission Plan (1946).
  • Strength: 389 members (292 British Provinces + 93 Princely States + 4 Chief Commissioners' Provinces).
  • Indirectly elected by provincial legislatures using proportional representation with single transferable vote.
  • First meeting: 9 December 1946. Temp. Chairman: Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha. Permanent Chairman: Dr. Rajendra Prasad.
  • Objective Resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru on 13 Dec 1946; adopted on 22 Jan 1947.
  • Drafting Committee: 8 members, chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (29 Aug 1947).
  • Total sessions: 11 (last on 24 Jan 1950). Total days: 165.
  • Adopted: 26 November 1949. Enforced: 26 January 1950 (Republic Day).

The Preamble

The Preamble declares India to be a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and secures to all citizens Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity.

Sovereignty — supreme power, no external authority.
Socialist (added by 42nd Amendment, 1976) — social & economic equality.
Secular (42nd Amendment, 1976) — no state religion.
Democratic — government by the people.
Republic — head of state elected, not hereditary.

Key words/philosophy: Justice (social, economic, political), Liberty (thought, expression, belief, faith), Equality (status and opportunity), Fraternity (dignity, unity).

Sources of Preamble: U.S. (Sovereign, Democratic, Republic), U.S.S.R. (Socialist), France (Liberty, Equality, Fraternity — motto).

Berubari Union Case (1960): Preamble is not a part of the Constitution. Overturned by LIC of India v. Consumer Education & Research Centre (1995) — Preamble is a part and can be amended under Article 368.

Sources of the Constitution

  • Government of India Act 1935 — federal scheme, emergency, public service commissions, judiciary.
  • British Constitution — parliamentary form, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, cabinet system.
  • US Constitution — fundamental rights, judicial review, federalism, independence of judiciary, impeachment.
  • Irish Constitution — DPSP, method of presidential election, nomination of Rajya Sabha members.
  • Canadian Constitution — federation with strong centre, residuary powers with centre, appointment of state governors.
  • Australian Constitution — concurrent list, joint sitting of Parliament, freedom of trade.
  • South African Constitution — procedure for constitutional amendment, election of Rajya Sabha members.
  • German Constitution — suspension of fundamental rights during emergency.
  • Japanese Constitution — procedure established by law.

Salient Features of the Constitution

  1. Lengthiest written constitution — originally 395 Articles + 8 Schedules + 22 Parts; currently 470+ Articles, 12 Schedules, 25 Parts.
  2. Blend of rigidity and flexibility — some provisions by special majority, others by simple majority.
  3. Federal system with unitary bias — strong centre, three lists (Union, State, Concurrent).
  4. Single citizenship (unlike US dual citizenship).
  5. Independent judiciary — Supreme Court at apex with wide jurisdiction.
  6. Fundamental Rights + DPSP + Fundamental Duties — unique combination.
  7. Universal adult suffrage — every citizen 18+ has the right to vote.
  8. Secular state — no official religion.

Fundamental Rights & Duties

Overview — Six Fundamental Rights

Part III (Articles 12–35). Originally 7 rights; Right to Property was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and made a legal right under Article 300A.

#RightArticles
1Right to Equality14–18
2Right to Freedom19–22
3Right against Exploitation23–24
4Right to Freedom of Religion25–28
5Cultural & Educational Rights29–30
6Right to Constitutional Remedies32
Dr. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "heart and soul of the Constitution" because it provides direct enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

  • Art. 14: Equality before law and equal protection of laws (borrowed from UK & US).
  • Art. 15: No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex. State may make special provisions for women, children, SCs, STs.
  • Art. 16: Equal opportunity in public employment. Reservations for SC/ST/OBC/EWS in govt. jobs.
  • Art. 17: Abolishes untouchability — punishable offence.
  • Art. 18: Abolishes titles (except military/academic). Rajya Sabha can be given titles — Bharat Ratna, Padma awards (these are awards, not titles).

Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)

  • Art. 19: Six freedoms — (a) speech & expression, (b) assembly, (c) association, (d) movement, (e) residence, (f) profession. Only available to citizens.
  • Art. 19(1)(a): Freedom of speech — subject to reasonable restrictions (Art. 19(2)): sovereignty, integrity, security, public order, decency, morality, contempt, defamation, incitement.
  • Art. 20: Protection against conviction for offences — (i) ex-post-facto law, (ii) double jeopardy, (iii) self-incrimination.
  • Art. 21: Right to Life and Personal Liberty — "no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law". Supreme Court has expanded this to include Right to Privacy (Puttaswamy 2017), Right to Education, Right to Clean Environment, Right to Livelihood, Right to Health.
  • Art. 21A: Right to free & compulsory education for children 6–14 (added by 86th Amendment).
  • Art. 22: Protection against arrest & detention — right to be informed of grounds, to consult & be defended by a lawyer, to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.
Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded Art. 21: "procedure" must be "just, fair and reasonable", not arbitrary.

Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

  • Art. 23: Prohibits traffic in human beings, forced labour (begar). Violation is punishable.
  • Art. 24: Prohibits employment of children below 14 years in factories, mines, hazardous employment.
Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 — passed to give effect to Art. 23.

Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

  • Art. 25: Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion. Subject to public order, morality, health, other fundamental rights.
  • Art. 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs — establish institutions, manage affairs, acquire property, administer.
  • Art. 27: No tax for promotion of any religion.
  • Art. 28: No religious instruction in state-funded educational institutions (exception allowed for institutions established under endowment/trust for religious purpose).

Cultural & Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

  • Art. 29: Protects interests of minorities — right to conserve language, script, culture. Any section with distinct language/culture has right to establish & administer educational institutions.
  • Art. 30: Right of all minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. State cannot discriminate in grant of aid on the basis of religion/linguistic minority status.

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

The dr. Ambedkar's "heart and soul" — guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. SC can issue five writs.

  • Available only when Fundamental Right is violated (unlike Art. 226 — High Court writ jurisdiction is wider).
  • Supreme Court is the guarantor of Fundamental Rights.
  • Art. 32(2): SC can issue directions/orders/writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, certiorari.
  • Art. 32(3): Parliament can empower other courts to exercise these powers.
Art. 32 vs 226: Both can issue writs. Art. 32 — only for FR violation; Art. 226 — for any legal right (wider scope). SC under Art. 32; HC under Art. 226.

Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

Added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee (1976). Originally 10 duties; 11th added by 86th Amendment (2002). They are non-justiciable — no court can enforce them directly, but Parliament may impose penalties for non-compliance.

  1. Abide by the Constitution, respect national flag & anthem.
  2. Cherish noble ideals of freedom struggle.
  3. Uphold sovereignty, unity and integrity.
  4. Defend the country, render national service when called.
  5. Promote common brotherhood, renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity.
  6. Value and preserve rich heritage of composite culture.
  7. Protect natural environment (forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife), have compassion for living creatures.
  8. Develop scientific temper, humanism, spirit of inquiry and reform.
  9. Safeguard public property, abjure violence.
  10. Strive for excellence in all spheres.
  11. (Added 2002) Provide opportunities for education to child 6–14 years (parents/guardians).

Directive Principles & Writs

Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)

Part IV (Articles 36–51). Borrowed from the Irish Constitution. They are non-justiciable (not legally enforceable), but fundamental in governance.

Art. 36: Definition of State — same as Art. 12.
Art. 37: Application of DPSP — fundamental in governance, duty of State to apply.
Art. 38: State to promote welfare of the people (social, economic, political justice).
Art. 39: Equal distribution of wealth, equal pay for equal work, prevention of concentration of wealth.
Art. 39A: Free legal aid (added by 42nd Amendment).
Art. 40: Organization of Village Panchayats.
Art. 41: Right to work, education, public assistance.
Art. 42: Just and humane conditions of work, maternity relief.
Art. 44: Uniform Civil Code (UCC).
Art. 45: Early childhood care (originally free & compulsory education for children; revised by 86th Amendment).
Art. 46: Promotion of education of SC/ST/weaker sections.
Art. 48: Organization of agriculture and animal husbandry; prohibition of cow slaughter.
Art. 48A: Protection of environment & wildlife (added by 42nd Amendment).
Art. 49: Protection of monuments and places of national importance.
Art. 50: Separation of judiciary from executive.
Art. 51: Promotion of international peace & security.

Classification of DPSP

CategoryExamples
Socialistic PrinciplesArt. 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 47 — aim at social & economic equality, welfare state.
Gandhian PrinciplesArt. 40 (Panchayats), 43 (Cottage Industries), 46 (SC/ST welfare), 48 (Cow slaughter prohibition).
Liberal-Intellectual PrinciplesArt. 44 (UCC), 45 (Education), 48 (Scientific agriculture), 50 (Separation of judiciary), 51 (International peace).
If any DPSP conflicts with a Fundamental Right, the Fundamental Right prevails (Champakam Dorairajan v. State of Madras, 1951). However, Parliament can amend FR to implement DPSP (under Art. 368).

The Minerva Mills case (1980) held that the harmony between FR and DPSP is the basic feature of the Constitution. FR cannot be totally subordinated to DPSP.

Writs — Five Types

WritMeaningAgainst WhomPurpose
Habeas Corpus"to have the body"State / private individual (for illegal detention)Release a person from illegal detention. Speedy remedy.
Mandamus"we command"Public authority / inferior court / govt. officialCompel performance of a public duty. Not against private person or the President/Governor.
Prohibition"to forbid"Inferior court / tribunalPrevent an inferior court from exceeding jurisdiction. Issued only during pendency of proceedings.
Certiorari"to be certified"Inferior court / tribunal / quasi-judicial bodyQuash an order already passed by inferior court acting beyond jurisdiction.
Quo Warranto"by what authority"Person holding public officeQuestion the authority of a person holding a public office. Used when office is created by statute.
Prohibition vs Certiorari: Both prevent excess of jurisdiction. Prohibition — preventive, before order is passed. Certiorari — curative, after order is passed.

Writ Jurisdiction

  • Art. 32: Supreme Court — for enforcement of Fundamental Rights only.
  • Art. 226: High Court — for Fundamental Rights and any other legal right. Wider scope.
  • Art. 227: High Court supervisory jurisdiction over subordinate courts.
Writs are prerogative writs, not rights. They are issued at the discretion of the court.

Union Government

The President (Articles 52–62)

  • Article 52: President of India is the head of state and head of the executive.
  • Article 54: Elected by an electoral college consisting of elected members of both Houses of Parliament + elected members of Legislative Assemblies of States + elected members of Legislative Assemblies of UTs (Delhi, Puducherry).
  • Article 61: Impeachment procedure — by either House on grounds of "violation of Constitution".
  • Article 62: Time of holding election to fill vacancy.
  • Term: 5 years, eligible for re-election.
  • Qualifications: Indian citizen, 35+ years, qualified to be Rajya Sabha member, not holding any office of profit.
  • Article 53: Executive power vests in President; exercised directly or through subordinate officers.
  • Veto Power: Absolute, Suspensive, Pocket (Art. 111).
  • Ordinance-making power: Art. 123 — when Parliament not in session. Must be approved by Parliament within 6 weeks of reassembly.
First President: Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1950–1962).
First (and only) Acting President: V.V. Giri (1969).
Shortest tenure: Dr. Rajendra Prasad served two terms.

Powers of the President

TypeExamples
ExecutiveAppoints PM, Ministers, Governors, Judges; chairs defence forces; receives ambassadors.
LegislativeSummons, prorogues Parliament; dissolves Lok Sabha; gives assent to bills; issues ordinances.
JudicialAppoints judges; pardons (Art. 72) — pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commutation, suspension.
FinancialLays Annual Budget; no money bill can be introduced without recommendation.
EmergencyNational Emergency (Art. 352), President's Rule (Art. 356), Financial Emergency (Art. 360).
All executive actions of the President are expressed to be taken in the name of the Governor-General of India (Art. 77). President acts on the aid & advice of the Council of Ministers (Art. 74, 42nd Amendment) — advice is binding.

The Vice President (Articles 63–71)

  • Article 63: Vice President of India.
  • Article 64: Ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha.
  • Article 65: Acts as President when post is vacant or President unable to discharge duties.
  • Elected by members of both Houses of Parliament (not state assemblies).
  • No impeachment — only resignation or removal by Rajya Sabha passing a resolution (assented to by Lok Sabha).

The Prime Minister & Council of Ministers

  • Article 75: Council of Ministers headed by PM to aid & advise President.
  • Article 78: Duties of PM — to communicate to President all decisions of the Council, furnish information, etc.
  • PM is appointed by the President; other ministers by President on PM's advice.
  • PM is the leader of the Lok Sabha (generally); Leader of the House.
  • Council of Ministers collectively responsible to Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)).
Three categories: Cabinet (senior, ~15–20 ministers), Minister of State (independent charge or assisting), Deputy Ministers.

Supreme Court of India (Articles 124–147)

  • Establishment: 28 January 1950 (inaugurated on Republic Day).
  • Composition: Chief Justice of India + 33 other judges (currently sanctioned strength; subject to Parliament's variation).
  • Appointment: Collegium system (since 1993) — recommendation by CJI + 4 senior-most judges; formally appointed by President. NJAC struck down (2015) as violating judicial independence.
  • Tenure: Till age 65.
  • Removal: By impeachment (procedure as in Parliament — 2/3 majority in each House).

Doctrine of Basic Structure (Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, 1973): Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a way that destroys its basic structure.

State Government

The Governor (Articles 152–167)

  • Article 153: Governor for each State. By 7th Amendment, same person can be Governor of two or more states.
  • Article 155: Appointed by the President.
  • Article 156: Holds office during the pleasure of the President (normally 5 years).
  • Article 154: Executive power of State vested in Governor.
  • Article 161: Pardoning power — pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commutation, suspension of sentence (for state offences).
  • Article 167: Duties of CM to furnish information.
Governor acts as constitutional head (like President at Union). Real executive power lies with CM & Council of Ministers.
Governor is appointed, not elected — thus differs from President.

Discretionary Powers of Governor

  • Appointing CM when no clear majority.
  • Dissolving State Legislative Assembly.
  • Recommending President's Rule (Art. 356).
  • Reserving bills for President's consideration (Art. 200).
  • Reporting to President on breakdown of constitutional machinery.
Article 356 — President's Rule is also called "State Emergency" or "Constitutional Emergency". Maximum duration: 1 year (extendable up to 3 years at 6-month intervals under specific conditions).

Chief Minister (Articles 163–164)

  • Appointed by the Governor.
  • Leader of the party/coalition with majority in Legislative Assembly.
  • Head of the Council of Ministers.
  • Chief coordinator, head of planning, leader of Legislative Assembly.
  • Holds office during Governor's pleasure (in effect during majority support).
  • Council of Ministers collectively responsible to Legislative Assembly (Art. 164(2)).

High Court (Articles 214–231)

  • Art. 214: High Court for each state. By 7th Amendment, one HC can serve multiple states/UTs.
  • Composition: Chief Justice + other judges. Strength determined by President.
  • Appointment: Collegium system. Appointed by President.
  • Tenure: Till age 62.
  • Removal: By President after address by Parliament on ground of misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Jurisdiction: Original (writs under Art. 226), Appellate (from subordinate courts), Supervisory (Art. 227), Advisory (to Governor).
Transfer of Judges: President, after consulting CJI, can transfer a HC judge to another HC.
First HC: Calcutta HC (1862).
Number of HCs: 25 (as of recent).
Article 226 vs Article 32: Art. 226 is wider — HC can issue writs for any legal right, not just FR.

Subordinate Courts

  • District Court: Highest court at district level. Judge appointed by Governor. Sessions judge handles criminal cases.
  • Subordinate Courts: Under District Court — Sub-Judge, Munsif Court, Civil Judge (Junior Division), Judicial Magistrate.
  • Hierarchy: Civil Judge → District Judge → High Court → Supreme Court.

Indian Knowledge Tradition (IKT)

Ancient Indian Education System — Gurukul

The Gurukul system was an ancient Indian residential schooling system where students (shishya) lived with their guru (teacher) and learned various subjects.

  • Guru-Shishya Parampara: Teacher-student tradition. Knowledge passed orally and through practice.
  • Subjects: Vedas, Upanishads, grammar (Vyakarana), logic, mathematics, medicine (Ayurveda), warfare, arts, philosophy, ethics (Dharma).
  • Methodology: Discussion (Samvad), debate (Vada), questioning, memorization, practical training.
  • Famous Centres: Takshashila (Taxila), Nalanda, Vikramshila, Kanchi, Vallabhi.
  • Values: Discipline, humility, reverence for nature, holistic development (physical, mental, spiritual).

Indian Contribution to Mathematics

  • Zero (Shunya): Attributed to Aryabhata (5th century) or Brahmagupta (7th century).
  • Decimal system & place value: Indian innovation.
  • Aryabhata (476–550 CE): Calculated value of π (~3.1416), explained eclipses, Earth's rotation.
  • Brahmagupta: Operations with zero, negative numbers, quadratic equations.
  • Bhaskara II (12th century): Calculus concepts (Lilavati), trigonometric functions.
  • Madhava of Sangamagrama: Infinite series for π and trigonometric functions (before Newton).
  • Ramanujan (1887–1920): Number theory, infinite series, mathematical analysis.

Indian Contribution to Science

  • Astronomy: Aryabhata's heliocentric theory, Surya Siddhanta, Pancha Siddhantika of Varahamihira.
  • Metallurgy: Iron Pillar of Delhi (rust-resistant), Wootz steel exported worldwide as "Damascus steel".
  • Shipbuilding: Stitched ship technology (Bhai-ka-Tanka), navigation by stars.
  • Agriculture: Crop rotation, irrigation techniques (Indus Valley), plough technology.
  • Civil Engineering: Stepwells (baoli), temple architecture, Indus Valley urban planning.

Indian Contribution to Medicine (Ayurveda)

  • Ayurveda = "science of life" — Atharvaveda's upaveda.
  • Charaka Samhita (Charaka, 1st century CE): Internal medicine. Called "Father of Indian Medicine".
  • Sushruta Samhita (Sushruta, 6th century BCE): Surgery. Rhinoplasty (plastic surgery), cataract surgery. "Father of Surgery".
  • Dhanvantari: Considered the god of Ayurveda.
  • Tridosha theory: Vata, Pitta, Kapha.
  • Yoga & wellness: Patanjali's system, meditation, pranayama.
Sushruta described rhinoplasty (nose reconstruction) — the first plastic surgery in the world — thousands of years before modern practice.

Languages & Literature

  • Sanskrit — classical language, source of many Indian languages. Panini's Ashtadhyayi — first formal grammar (4th century BCE).
  • Pali — language of Buddhist scriptures.
  • Prakrit — language of Jain texts.
  • Tamil — Sangam literature (300 BCE–300 CE), Thirukkural, Silappatikaram.
  • Other classical languages: Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Odia — recognized by Sahitya Akademi.

Local Governance

Panchayati Raj (73rd Amendment, 1992)

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992) gave Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) constitutional status. Added Part IX (Articles 243–243O) and 11th Schedule (29 subjects).

  • Three-tier system: Village (Gram Panchayat) → Block (Panchayat Samiti / Intermediate Panchayat) → District (Zilla Parishad).
  • Article 243D: All seats filled by direct election. Reservation of seats for SC/ST in proportion to population.
  • Article 243D(3): One-third of total seats reserved for women (not below). 50% reservation for women under 73rd & 74th Amendment.
  • Article 243E: Duration of Panchayat — 5 years; dissolution before expiry invokes fresh elections within 6 months.
  • Article 243F: Qualifications, disqualifications for membership — determined by State Election Commission.
  • Article 243H: Powers to levy taxes.
  • Article 243J: State Finance Commission every 5 years.

Historical Background of Panchayati Raj

  • Mahatma Gandhi's vision: Village republics as base of Indian democracy.
  • Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957): Recommended three-tier Panchayati Raj system.
  • Rajasthan (1959): First state to implement Panchayati Raj (Nagaur district — Oct 2, 1959).
  • Ashok Mehta Committee (1977): Recommended two-tier system, district as basic unit.
  • 73rd Amendment (1992): Gave constitutional status to Panchayats.
  • Article 40 (DPSP): Already directed state to organize Village Panchayats.
The 73rd Amendment is not applicable to: (a) Jammu & Kashmir (had its own provision), (b) States with population < 20 lakh (exempted), (c) Scheduled Areas (Article 243M) — governed by PESA (1996).

Municipalities (74th Amendment, 1992)

The 74th Constitutional Amendment (1992) gave Municipalities constitutional status. Added Part IX-A (Articles 243P–243ZG) and 12th Schedule (18 subjects).

  • Three types: Nagar Panchayat (smaller area), Municipal Council (smaller urban area), Municipal Corporation (larger urban area).
  • Article 243Q: Constitution of municipalities in states.
  • Article 243R: Composition of municipalities — direct elections.
  • Article 243T: Duration — 5 years.
  • Article 243W: Power to impose taxes.
  • Article 243X: State Finance Commission.
12th Schedule (18 subjects): Urban planning, regulation of land use, roads & bridges, water supply, public health, sanitation, drainage, solid waste management, fire services, urban forestry, slum clearance, urban poverty alleviation, parks, gardens, cultural activities, etc.

Common Exam Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing Article 32 (SC — only FR) with Article 226 (HC — wider, any legal right).

  • Confusing 11th Schedule (29 subjects) with 12th Schedule (18 subjects).
  • Writing "Preamble is not part of Constitution" without noting it was overturned by LIC v. Consumer Education (1995).
  • Confusing Prohibition (preventive) with Certiorari (curative).
  • Forgetting the speaker presides over joint sitting, not the Vice President.
  • Mixing up Rajya Sabha (perpetual body) with Lok Sabha (dissolvable).
  • Confusing Panchayat Samiti (block level) with Gram Panchayat (village level).

  • Confusing "Socialist" and "Secular" as part of original Preamble — both added by 42nd Amendment (1976).

Article 32. Dr. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul of the Constitution."

Q: Who was the Chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Constituent Assembly?

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, appointed on 29 August 1947.

Q: Which Amendment added the words "Socialist" and "Secular" to the Preamble?

Q: Which Article abolishes untouchability?

Q: Who moved the Objective Resolution in the Constituent Assembly and on what date?

Jawaharlal Nehru moved it on 13 December 1946. It was adopted on 22 January 1947.

Q: Which Article provides for the Right to Privacy?

Article 21, as declared by the Supreme Court in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017).

Q: What is the term of office of the President of India?

5 years from the date of entering office. Eligible for re-election.

Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto.

Q: What is the minimum age required for membership of the Lok Sabha?

Group B — Five Mark Questions

Q: Explain the concept of "Secularism" as embodied in the Indian Constitution. How is it different from the Western concept?

Indian Secularism means the State has no official religion and treats all religions with equal respect. The word "Secular" was inserted in the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976). Under Articles 25-28, every citizen has the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate any religion. Article 27 prohibits the State from levying any tax for the promotion of any religion. Unlike the Western model which advocates a strict "wall of separation" between religion and State, Indian secularism allows the State to intervene in religious affairs for reform — abolishing untouchability, banning child marriage, allowing Dalits into temples. Key principles are: (i) Sarva Dharma Sambhava — equal respect for all religions, (ii) no state religion, (iii) freedom of conscience, (iv) prohibition on religious instruction in state-funded educational institutions.

Q: Compare and contrast the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha with reference to their composition, term, and powers.

Composition: Lok Sabha has 543 elected members (max 552) with minimum age 25. Rajya Sabha has 250 members (max) — 12 nominated by President, 238 elected by state assemblies, minimum age 30. Term: Lok Sabha has a 5-year term and is dissolvable by the President. Rajya Sabha is a permanent body; one-third retire every 2 years and it cannot be dissolved. Money Bills: Can only originate in Lok Sabha. Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations within 14 days; Lok Sabha may accept or reject them. Speaker: Lok Sabha has a Speaker and Deputy Speaker. Rajya Sabha is presided over by the Vice President (ex-officio Chairman) and has a Deputy Chairman. Power: Lok Sabha is more powerful. In joint sittings (Art. 108), Lok Sabha's larger number usually determines the outcome. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible only to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)).

Q: Classify the Directive Principles of State Policy with relevant article numbers and examples.

DPSP (Part IV, Articles 36-51) are classified into three categories. (1) Socialistic Principles — Art. 38 (promote welfare), Art. 39 (equal distribution of wealth), Art. 39A (free legal aid), Art. 41 (right to work), Art. 42 (maternity relief). (2) Gandhian Principles — Art. 40 (village panchayats), Art. 43 (cottage industries), Art. 46 (SC/ST welfare), Art. 48 (prohibition of cow slaughter). (3) Liberal-Intellectual Principles — Art. 44 (Uniform Civil Code), Art. 45 (early childhood care and education), Art. 48A (environment protection), Art. 50 (separation of judiciary from executive), Art. 51 (international peace). If DPSP conflicts with a Fundamental Right, the Fundamental Right prevails (Champakam Dorairajan, 1951). The Minerva Mills case (1980) held that harmony between FR and DPSP is a basic feature of the Constitution.

Q: List and explain the Fundamental Duties under Article 51A. Why are they considered non-justiciable?

The Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A) were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Originally 10 duties, the 11th was added by the 86th Amendment (2002): (1) abide by the Constitution, respect national flag and anthem; (2) cherish noble ideals of freedom struggle; (3) uphold sovereignty, unity, and integrity; (4) defend the country; (5) promote common brotherhood; (6) value and preserve composite culture; (7) protect natural environment; (8) develop scientific temper; (9) safeguard public property, abjure violence; (10) strive for excellence; (11) provide opportunities for education to children aged 6-14. They are non-justiciable because Art. 37 (applying to DPSP) equally applies to Fundamental Duties — no court can enforce them directly. However, Parliament may by law impose penalties for non-compliance. They serve as a moral reminder that rights and duties go hand in hand.

Q: Describe the Panchayati Raj system introduced by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment. What are its key features?

The 73rd Amendment (1992) gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj Institutions by adding Part IX (Articles 243-243O) and the 11th Schedule (29 subjects). Three-tier structure — Gram Panchayat at village level, Panchayat Samiti at block level, Zilla Parishad at district level. Art. 243D mandates direct elections for all seats and reservation for SCs/STs in proportion to population. Art. 243D(3) mandates one-third reservation for women. Art. 243E fixes the duration at 5 years. Art. 243H provides powers to levy taxes. Art. 243J establishes a State Finance Commission every 5 years. The 11th Schedule covers 29 subjects including agriculture, minor irrigation, drinking water, rural roads, housing, electrification, education, health, and public distribution system. The Amendment is not applicable to Scheduled Areas (governed by PESA, 1996).

Q: What is the Doctrine of Basic Structure? Explain with reference to the Kesavananda Bharati case and its significance.

The Doctrine of Basic Structure holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a way that destroys or alters its basic structure. This was propounded by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) by a 7-6 majority. The Court held that Parliament's amending power under Art. 368 is not unlimited. The basic structure includes: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, unity and integrity, sovereignty, free and fair elections, independence of judiciary, rule of law, and harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSP. The doctrine was reinforced in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), which held that the balance between FR and DPSP is part of the basic structure. This doctrine serves as a judicial check against potential abuse of parliamentary power.

Q: Explain the significance of the Iron Pillar of Delhi as a testament to ancient Indian metallurgical knowledge.

The Iron Pillar of Delhi, located near the Qutub Minar, stands as a remarkable testament to ancient Indian metallurgical prowess. Erected around 400 CE during the Gupta period, the 7-meter tall pillar is made of wrought iron weighing approximately 6 tonnes and has not rusted for over 1,600 years despite Delhi's extreme climate. The secret lies in the composition of the iron — a high phosphorus content (about 0.25%) combined with low sulfur and manganese, which created a protective film of hydrogen phosphate on the surface, preventing corrosion. The pillar bears an inscription in Brahmi script mentioning a king named Chandra, believed to be Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya). This demonstrates that ancient Indian metallurgists had advanced knowledge of corrosion-resistant materials long before similar technology emerged in Europe. It remains one of the greatest metallurgical achievements in human history and a proud symbol of Indian Knowledge Tradition (IKT).

Group C — Fifteen Mark Questions

Q: Discuss the historical background leading to the making of the Indian Constitution. Explain in detail the role of the Constituent Assembly in drafting the Constitution.

Historical Background: Before independence, India was governed by a series of British laws. The Government of India Act, 1858 abolished the East India Company's rule and transferred power to the British Crown, creating the post of Viceroy. The Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto) introduced separate electorates for Muslims. The Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford) introduced dyarchy in provinces, bicameral legislature, and separate provincial budgets. The Government of India Act, 1935 proposed an All-India Federation, provincial autonomy, and division of powers into three lists (Union, State, Concurrent) — about 75% of the Indian Constitution is borrowed from this Act. The Indian Independence Act, 1947 provided for partition and declared Constituent Assemblies as sovereign bodies.

Constituent Assembly: Formed based on the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) with 389 members. The first meeting was held on 9 December 1946, with Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha as temporary Chairman. Dr. Rajendra Prasad became the permanent Chairman. On 13 December 1946, Jawaharlal Nehru moved the historic "Objective Resolution," adopted on 22 January 1947. A Drafting Committee of 8 members was formed on 29 August 1947, chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, known as the "Father of the Indian Constitution." The Assembly met for 11 sessions over 165 days. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 (Constitution Day) and came into force on 26 January 1950 (Republic Day). The choice of 26 January was symbolic — it commemorated the declaration of Purna Swaraj in 1930. Key contributors included Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel, Rajendra Prasad, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, and Alladi Krishnaswamy Iyer. Dr. Ambedkar, as Chairman of the Drafting Committee, guided the framing of the Constitution's 470+ Articles and 12 Schedules with extraordinary legal acumen and vision for social justice.

Q: Explain the Fundamental Rights under Part III of the Constitution. Discuss the significance of Article 21 and its judicial expansion.

Fundamental Rights (Articles 12-35) are basic human rights enforceable by courts. Originally seven rights; Right to Property was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and made a legal right under Art. 300A. Six Fundamental Rights: (1) Right to Equality (Arts. 14-18): Art. 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws. Art. 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Art. 16 guarantees equal opportunity in public employment. Art. 17 abolishes untouchability. Art. 18 abolishes titles. (2) Right to Freedom (Arts. 19-22): Art. 19 grants six freedoms — speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession. Art. 20 protects against ex-post-facto laws, double jeopardy, and self-incrimination. (3) Right against Exploitation (Arts. 23-24): Art. 23 prohibits human trafficking and forced labour. Art. 24 prohibits child labour below 14 in hazardous work. (4) Right to Freedom of Religion (Arts. 25-28): Freedom of conscience, profession, practice, and propagation of religion. (5) Cultural and Educational Rights (Arts. 29-30): Protects minority interests. (6) Right to Constitutional Remedies (Art. 32): Guarantees enforcement through five writs.

Article 21 — Judicial Expansion: Art. 21 states no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court held the procedure must be "just, fair, and reasonable." In K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), Right to Privacy was declared a fundamental right under Art. 21. Other expanded rights include: Right to Education (later Art. 21A), Right to Clean Environment, Right to Livelihood, Right to Health, Right to Speedy Trial, Right to Shelter, Right to Dignity, and Right to Die with Dignity (passive euthanasia — Common Cause v. Union of India, 2018). Dr. Ambedkar called Art. 32 the "heart and soul" of the Constitution, but the judicial expansion of Art. 21 makes it the most significant fundamental right today.

Q: Describe the Union Government under the Constitution — the President, Prime Minister, Parliament, and Supreme Court.

The President (Articles 52-62): The President is the head of state and executive, elected by an electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs of all states and UTs. Term is 5 years, minimum age 35. Powers include: Executive (appoints PM, Ministers, Governors, Judges), Legislative (summons/prorogues Parliament, gives assent to bills, issues ordinances under Art. 123), Judicial (pardons under Art. 72 — pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commutation, suspension), Financial (lays budget), and Emergency (Arts. 352, 356, 360). Veto power includes absolute, suspensive, and pocket veto. However, the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Art. 74), which is binding.

The Prime Minister (Art. 75): The PM is appointed by the President (leader of majority party in Lok Sabha). Other ministers are appointed on PM's advice. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Three categories: Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, and Deputy ministers. The PM is the chief coordinator, leader of the House, and the primary link between the President and the government.

Parliament: Consists of President + two Houses. Lok Sabha (543 members, 25+ age, 5-year term, dissolvable, more powerful). Rajya Sabha (250 members, 30+ age, permanent body, one-third retire every 2 years). Joint sittings under Art. 108 are presided over by the Speaker of Lok Sabha. Money Bills can only originate in Lok Sabha.

Supreme Court (Articles 124-147): Established 28 January 1950. Composed of CJI + up to 33 judges. Judges appointed by Collegium system, serve till age 65. Removal by impeachment. Jurisdictions: Original (Art. 131 — Centre-State disputes), Appellate (from HCs), Advisory (Art. 143), Writ (Art. 32 for FR enforcement), and Review (Art. 137). Law declared by SC is binding on all courts (Art. 141).

Q: Discuss the Indian Knowledge Tradition comprehensively — covering the Gurukul system, Vedic literature, the six Darshanas, and India's contributions to science, mathematics, and medicine.

Gurukul System: The ancient Indian residential schooling system was based on the Guru-Shishya Parampara, where students lived with their guru and learned through direct interaction. Subjects included the Vedas, Upanishads, grammar (Vyakarana), logic, mathematics, medicine (Ayurveda), warfare, arts, and philosophy. Famous centres included Takshashila (~700 BCE) — arguably the world's first university attracting students from China, Greece, and Persia — as well as Nalanda (5th century CE), Vikramshila, Kanchi, and Vallabhi. Teaching methods included discussion (Samvad), debate (Vada), and questioning. Values imparted were discipline, humility, reverence for nature, and holistic development.

Vedas and Upanishads: The four Vedas are: Rigveda (~1500 BCE, oldest, 1028 hymns), Yajurveda (sacrificial formulas), Samaveda (source of Indian classical music), and Atharvaveda (spells, healing, daily life). The ~108 Upanishads explore philosophical meanings of the Vedas. The Vedangas (limbs) are: Shiksha (phonetics), Kalpa (rituals), Vyakarana (grammar), Nirukta (etymology), Chanda (metrics), and Jyotisha (astronomy).

Six Darshanas: (1) Samkhya (Kapila) — dualism of Purusha (consciousness) and Prakriti (matter), 25 tattvas; (2) Yoga (Patanjali) — Ashtanga Yoga with 8 limbs: Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi; (3) Nyaya (Gautama) — logic, four pramanas (Pratyaksha, Anumana, Upamana, Shabda); (4) Vaisheshika (Kanada) — atomism (Paramanu); (5) Mimamsa (Jaimini) — ritual interpretation; (6) Vedanta (Badarayana) — Advaita (Shankaracharya), Dvaita (Madhvacharya), Vishishtadvaita (Ramanujacharya).

Contributions to Science and Mathematics: India invented the decimal system and zero (Shunya). Aryabhata calculated pi (~3.1416), explained eclipses, and proposed Earth's rotation. Brahmagupta defined operations with zero. Bhaskara II worked on calculus. Madhava developed infinite series for pi. In medicine, Sushruta Samhita described rhinoplasty (first plastic surgery) and cataract surgery. Charaka Samhita is the foundational text of Ayurveda. The Iron Pillar of Delhi demonstrates rust-resistant metallurgy. Panini's Ashtadhyayi (4th century BCE) is the world's first formal grammar. Sangam Tamil literature (Thirukkural, Silappatikaram) represents one of the world's oldest literary traditions.
